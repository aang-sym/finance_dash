My turn on the flop.

To call is $4. I've tapped four chips off my rail — two $5 and two $1 — so the confirm button has become Raise to $12. Nothing is committed until I press it.

  1. Pot at the top, my chips at the bottom. Betting is physically pushing money away from me. That directionality is most of the metaphor and it's free — it costs one decision about layout order.
  2. The pot is a recess, not a card. Inset shadow and a darker felt gradient, so chips move down into shadow when they're bet. Tapping it zooms the camera in rather than opening a modal.
  3. Your turn is a 3px strip at the top edge — no banner, no space cost, impossible to miss. The incumbent spends a full-width black bar saying Your turn · Flop · You can check; that information is already carried by the strip, the live action bar and the board.
  4. Opponents show committed chips, not just numbers. Mike's $4 sits in front of him as an actual chip between him and the pot. You read the hand by looking at where the money is.
  5. Four denominations, because that's what a $50 buy-in actually holds. Showing $500 chips in a $1/$2 game would be theatre. The rail is the real contents of your stack.
  6. No number pad and no slider. Tap a stack, get a chip. The count is the bet. A slider is faster and much less fun, and fun is the entire reason to rebuild this.
Feel — what fires where

Every rail cell and action button carries an invisible native <input type="checkbox" switch> across its hit area, so a real finger tap gets a real Taptic tick on iOS. Four taps to build $12 is four ticks and four clinks with rising pitch — the one haptic pattern iOS still permits, since each tick is its own direct tap.

Pull back, the pot zoom, and anything triggered by another player get no haptic on iOS by design — script-driven ticks stopped working in 26.5. Those moments lean on audio and the table kick instead.
